## EU textile EPR in brief

Extended producer responsibility, or EPR, is a simple idea. The business that sells a product pays for what happens to it once it is thrown away. The EU already uses EPR for packaging, batteries and electronics. Directive (EU) 2025/1892 now brings EU textile EPR to clothing, footwear and household textiles in every EU country.

This guide is for brands that sell clothing in the EU. It explains who pays, when the rules start and what to ask your factory for. It is general information, not legal advice, so confirm the details with an adviser in each country where you sell.

## The deadlines that matter

| Date | What happens |
|---|---|
| 26 September 2025 | Directive (EU) 2025/1892 published in the Official Journal |
| 17 October 2025 | The Directive enters into force |
| 17 June 2027 | Deadline for EU countries to write the rules into national law |
| 17 April 2028 | Deadline for every EU country to have a textile EPR scheme running |

France has run a textile EPR scheme for years, and other countries such as the Netherlands started early too. If you already sell there, you may be registered. Other countries will add schemes through 2027 and 2028, each with its own fees and forms.

## Who counts as the producer

Under extended producer responsibility for textiles, the producer is the business that first makes the clothing available on a country's market. In practice that is usually one of these:

- **The brand or importer** that ships the clothing into that EU country.
- **The online seller** outside the EU that ships direct to consumers there.
- **The retailer** that buys from a supplier outside the EU and sells under its own name.

A garment factory in Bangladesh that sells FOB to a brand is not the producer. The brand pays the fees, files the reports and keeps the records. That is why EPR planning starts with your own sales data, not your factory.

## What EPR registration involves

EPR registration works country by country. Expect these steps in each EU country where you sell clothing:

1. **Register** with the national scheme or a producer responsibility organisation.
2. **Report** the quantity or weight of textiles you place on that market, usually each year.
3. **Pay fees** that fund collection, sorting, reuse and recycling.
4. **Appoint a representative** if you sell there from outside the country and the rules ask for one.
5. **Keep records** that match your invoices and shipping documents.

Many brands use a compliance service that handles several countries at once. Ask what data it needs, because your factory may need to supply part of it.

## Eco-modulated fees and product design

The Directive asks schemes to use eco-modulated fees. The fee for each garment changes with how it is designed and made. Clothing that lasts longer, can be repaired and is easy to recycle is expected to pay less. Garments that are hard to reuse or recycle are expected to pay more.

Each country will set its own criteria. These are the design choices most likely to matter:

- **Durability:** strong seams, good colour fastness and fabric that resists pilling.
- **Fibre mix:** a single fibre or a simple blend is easier to recycle than a complex mix.
- **Trims:** removable or fewer metal and plastic trims help sorting and recycling.
- **Information:** clear fibre content and care labels, so sorters know what they have.

## What to ask your factory for

Your factory cannot register for you, but it holds data you will need. Ask for these on every order:

- Fibre composition for each fabric and trim, from the mill's report.
- Garment weight per size, which some schemes use to calculate fees.
- Country of manufacture and the factory's details.
- Mill certificates for any recycled or organic content you plan to claim.
- Care label wording that matches the fabric tests.

## EPR and other EU rules

EU textile EPR is one part of a wider set of rules. The ban on destroying unsold clothing, new packaging rules and the future digital product passport all touch the same products.
