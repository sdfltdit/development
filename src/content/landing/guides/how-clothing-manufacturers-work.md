## Why it helps to know how a factory works

Most buyers only see emails, samples and cartons. Knowing how clothing manufacturers work behind the scenes helps you write better briefs, ask better questions and understand why some requests take longer than others.

## Garment factory departments

A full garment factory has several departments, each with one job. Your order passes through all of them.

| Department | What it does | What it needs from you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Merchandising | Quotes, plans and follows your order | A clear brief and quick replies |
| Sampling and patterns | Makes patterns, grades sizes, sews samples | Tech pack, size chart, fit comments |
| Fabric and trims store | Receives, checks and stores materials | Approved fabric, colours and trims |
| Cutting | Lays fabric and cuts panels | Approved pattern and marker |
| Printing and embroidery | Decorates panels or garments | Approved artwork and strike-offs |
| Sewing | Joins panels on production lines | Approved pre-production sample |
| Finishing | Trims threads, presses, attaches labels | Label and packing instructions |
| Quality | Checks every stage and runs final inspection | Your standards and tolerances |
| Packing | Folds, bags, tags and cartons goods | Packing list and carton marks |

## The merchandiser role

The merchandiser is your main contact. In most factories, the merchandiser role covers:

- turning your brief into a costed quote
- booking fabric, trims and production time
- passing your comments to the sampling room and lines
- keeping the time and action calendar on track
- sending updates, photos and reports

A good merchandiser saves you weeks. Give them complete information and fast answers, and they can do their best work.

## How an order moves through the factory

1. **Quote.** The merchandiser prices fabric, trims, sewing time and decoration.
2. **Sampling.** The pattern maker drafts the pattern and the sample room sews the proto.
3. **Approval.** You comment, the sample is corrected, and a pre-production sample is approved.
4. **Materials.** Fabric and trims arrive and are checked.
5. **Cutting.** Fabric is laid in layers and cut into panels by size.
6. **Decoration.** Panels or garments are printed or embroidered.
7. **Sewing.** Panels move along the production line.
8. **Finishing.** Threads are trimmed, garments pressed and labelled.
9. **Inspection.** Quality checks and a final random inspection.
10. **Packing and shipment.** Goods are packed, marked and shipped.

## The production line

A garment is broken into operations, such as joining shoulders, setting sleeves and hemming. On a production line, each operator does one or a few operations, and the garment moves from station to station.

Line set-up takes time. The line must be balanced so no station holds up the rest. That set-up time is one reason factories set minimum orders. Our guide on [what does MOQ mean](/insights/moq-meaning-clothing/) explains more.

## Factory capacity

Factory capacity is how many pieces a factory can make in a given time. It depends on:

- the number of production lines and operators
- the minutes it takes to sew your style
- how many other orders are booked

A simple tee takes far fewer minutes than a lined jacket. That is why lead times differ by product, and why booking early in a busy season matters.

## How factories price an order

| Cost part | What it covers |
| --- | --- |
| Fabric | Usually the largest part of the price |
| Trims and labels | Zips, buttons, labels, tags, packaging |
| Labour | Sewing minutes times the factory's cost per minute |
| Decoration | Printing, embroidery, washing or dyeing |
| Overheads | Building, power, management, testing |
| Margin | The factory's profit |

Quantity changes the price, because set-up costs are shared by more pieces.

## Different types of manufacturer

Not every company called a manufacturer runs all these departments. Some only sew fabric you supply. Some are trading companies that place orders with other factories.
