## Why clothing manufacturer scams work

Most clothing manufacturer scams target the same weak spot: a buyer far away who cannot see the factory and has to pay before seeing the goods. Scammers use urgency, low prices and copied emails to rush that buyer.

The good news is that a few simple checks stop most of them. None needs special tools.

## The six most common scams

| Scam | How it works | How to stop it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fake factory | A reseller claims to own a factory, then passes your order on | Ask what is made on their own lines and see the floor |
| Bait price | A very low quote, then extra charges after you pay | Get every cost on one written quote |
| Sample switch | A great sample, then weaker bulk | Approve a pre-production sample and inspect bulk |
| Bank detail fraud | An email says the bank account has changed | Confirm any change by phone on a known number |
| Deposit and vanish | A seller takes a deposit and disappears | Check the company and start with a small paid sample |
| Design theft | Your design is sold to others | Share files with care and sign an NDA |

## The fake clothing factory

A fake clothing factory is usually a trading company or agent presenting itself as the maker. That is not always a scam. Agents can be useful. The problem is the lie, because you lose control of who makes your goods and what they pay.

Ask:

- Which products do you sew on your own lines, and which go elsewhere?
- Can I see recent photos or video of the production floor with my style on it?
- What is the registered company name, and does it match the invoice?

## Payment fraud

Payment fraud through email is one of the costliest frauds in business. The FBI's internet crime unit, IC3, describes business email compromise as a sophisticated scam targeting businesses and people who make legitimate transfers. A common version targets buyers who pay foreign suppliers.

It usually looks like this:

1. A criminal gains access to, or copies, a supplier's email.
2. They watch real conversations about an order.
3. At payment time, they send an email saying the bank details have changed.
4. The buyer pays the new account, and the money is gone.

**How to protect yourself:**

- Never accept new bank details by email alone
- Call the supplier on a number you already have, not one in the email
- Check the account name matches the company on the proforma invoice
- Be suspicious of urgency, such as "pay today or lose your slot"

## How to verify a manufacturer

Before your first payment, verify a manufacturer with these checks:

| Check | What to look for |
| --- | --- |
| Registration | A registered company you can look up |
| Address | A real street address, ideally visited or shown on a live call |
| Floor | Recent, specific photos or video of production |
| References | Other buyers who will speak to you |
| Written terms | One quote with price, sample fee, minimum, lead time and payment schedule |
| Bank account | Account name matches the company on the invoice |

## Red flags in quotes

Watch for these red flags in quotes:

- a price far below every other quote, with no breakdown
- no sample, or pressure to skip it
- no written lead time
- payment requested in full before production
- payment to a personal account or a payment app
- answers that avoid your direct questions

One red flag can have an innocent reason. Several together are a reason to walk away.

## Protect yourself with structure

The best protection is a payment structure tied to things you can see:

1. **Pay for a sample first.** Judge quality and communication.
2. **Pay a deposit to start.** Not the full order.
3. **Approve a pre-production sample** in bulk fabric.
4. **Inspect before the final payment.** Our guide to [AQL inspection](/insights/aql-inspection-explained/) explains how.

Our guide to [payment terms for clothing orders](/insights/payment-terms-lc-vs-tt/) compares T/T and letters of credit.

## If something goes wrong

- Contact your bank straight away. Fast action gives the best chance of recalling a payment.
- Keep every email, invoice and message.
- Report it to the police or your national fraud reporting service.
- Warn your other suppliers if your own email may be compromised.
