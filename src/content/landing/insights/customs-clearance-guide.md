## What customs clearance means

Every shipment of clothing must clear customs before it can enter your country. Customs checks what the goods are, where they were made and what they are worth. Then it charges duty and tax and releases the goods.

This customs clearance guide walks through the process for brands buying from an overseas factory. Rules differ by country, so treat it as an outline and confirm details with your broker.

## The customs clearance process step by step

1. **The factory ships the goods** and sends the shipping documents.
2. **Your customs broker prepares the entry,** using the invoice, packing list and transport document.
3. **The goods are classified** under a tariff code, which sets the duty rate.
4. **Customs reviews the entry.** Most shipments pass on paper. Some are held for a physical check.
5. **You pay duty and tax,** usually through your broker.
6. **Customs releases the goods,** and your forwarder delivers them.

## What a customs broker does

A customs broker is a firm that handles import paperwork for you. A good customs broker will:

- check your documents before the goods arrive
- choose the right tariff code for each product
- claim lower duty where a trade deal or preference applies
- file the entry and pay duty on your behalf
- answer questions from customs and arrange any checks

Most freight forwarders offer customs brokerage, or can recommend a broker.

## The commercial invoice for clothing

The commercial invoice for clothing is the most important document. Customs uses it to set duty. A good one shows:

| Field | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Description | Men's knitted cotton T-shirt, crew neck |
| Fibre content | 100% cotton |
| Construction | Knitted or woven |
| Quantity | Pieces per style and colour |
| Unit and total value | In the agreed currency |
| Trade term | Such as FOB Chittagong |
| Country of origin | Bangladesh |

Vague descriptions such as "garments" or "samples" are a common reason for holds.

## Importer registration

You need an importer number before your first shipment:

- **UK and EU.** An EORI number, which starts with your country code.
- **United States.** An importer number, often your tax ID, plus a customs bond for most formal entries.
- **Canada and Australia.** A business number or registration with the customs authority.

Your broker can tell you what your country needs. Apply early, because getting an EORI number or a bond can take a few days.

## Duty, tax and trade deals

Duty depends on the tariff code, the country of origin and any trade deal. A certificate of origin can lower or remove duty where a deal or preference applies. Import tax, such as VAT or GST, is charged on top in many countries.

Our guide on [how to calculate landed cost](/insights/how-to-calculate-landed-cost/) shows how duty and tax add to your cost per piece.

## Samples and small parcels

Rules for small parcels changed in 2025 and 2026:

- **United States.** Duty-free entry for low-value commercial shipments is suspended. Samples by courier now need a formal or informal entry and may pay duty.
- **European Union.** Since 1 July 2026, a flat €3 duty per item applies to low-value parcels sent to consumers, until a permanent system starts in 2028. Business shipments follow normal rules.

Plan for duty and courier fees on samples, and make sure the courier invoice describes them clearly.

## How to avoid customs delays

Most customs delays come from paperwork, not the goods. To avoid them:

1. **Get your importer number** before the goods ship.
2. **Ask for draft documents** and check them before shipment.
3. **Use clear descriptions** with fibre content and knit or woven.
4. **Match the invoice value** to what you paid.
5. **Label goods correctly,** with country of origin and fibre content on sewn-in labels.
6. **Send documents early** to your broker, before the vessel arrives.
