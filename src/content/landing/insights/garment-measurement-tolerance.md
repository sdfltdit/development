## Garment measurement tolerance in brief

No two garments come off a sewing line exactly the same. Fabric relaxes, seams shift slightly and washing shrinks some styles. Garment measurement tolerance is the agreed range that allows for this, so a garment can be a little over or under the size chart and still pass.

Without a tolerance, quality checks become an argument. With one, the factory knows the target and the inspector knows when to fail a piece.

## How tolerance works

Every point of measure in a size chart has a target and a tolerance. If the half chest target for a medium is 52 cm with a tolerance of ±1 cm, any garment from 51 to 53 cm passes.

Tolerances apply to the measurement as written. A half chest tolerance of ±1 cm allows ±2 cm around the full body. Be clear whether your chart uses half or full measurements. Our guide on [how to create a tech pack](/how-to-create-a-tech-pack/) explains points of measure in detail.

## When to set wider or tighter tolerances

Some garments need more room, and some need less.

- **Washed and garment-dyed styles:** shrinkage varies from batch to batch, so length and chest often need ±0.5 cm more.
- **Stretch fabric:** leggings and activewear can measure differently each time, so agree how much tension to use.
- **Tailoring and denim waists:** fit is critical, so keep these tight, often ±0.5 cm.
- **Loose styles:** oversized tees and relaxed trousers can take wider length tolerances.
- **Small details:** pocket and label placement stay tight, because shoppers see them straight away.

Our [fabric shrinkage calculator](/tools/shrinkage-calculator/) helps you plan for shrinkage before setting a tolerance.

## How to measure garments flat

Most disputes come from two people measuring the same garment differently. Agree one method and write it down.

1. Lay the garment flat on a smooth table.
2. Close zips, buttons and snaps.
3. Smooth out wrinkles without stretching the fabric.
4. Measure each point exactly as your tech pack describes it, such as 2.5 cm below the armhole for half chest.
5. Use a fibreglass tape that does not stretch, and replace it if it is worn.
6. Measure knits relaxed, unless your tech pack says to stretch them.

Measure garments flat after pressing and before packing, the same way at the factory and at your warehouse.

## Taking the half chest measurement

The half chest measurement is the most checked point on any top. Measure it straight across from side seam to side seam, usually 2.5 cm below the armhole. Doubling it gives the full chest.

Many size problems trace back to a vague chest point. If your chart says only "chest", the factory and the inspector may each pick a different line. Draw the point on your flat sketch.

## Tolerance and AQL inspection

During a final inspection, the inspector measures a set number of garments from each size. Any point outside tolerance is recorded as a defect. The number of defects decides pass or fail under the AQL table, as our guide to [AQL inspection](/insights/aql-inspection-explained/) explains.

Critical points, such as chest or waist, are often treated as major defects. Small detail points may count as minor ones. Agree this split before bulk.

## Size tolerance across the size range

Tolerance applies to every size, not just the base. That is why grading matters. If each size is graded correctly, the same size tolerance holds from XS to 3XL. Our guide to [garment grading](/insights/garment-grading-explained/) shows how size steps are set, and a [size set sample](/insights/types-of-garment-samples/) checks them in real garments.
