## Garment testing in brief

A garment can look perfect in the factory and fail in a customer's washing machine. Garment testing catches that before bulk ships. Labs test fabric and finished pieces to set methods, then grade the results against the brand's standard.

This guide covers the tests brands ask for most, the methods behind them and typical pass grades. Every brand sets its own standard, so treat the figures as common practice, not rules.

## The main garment tests

| Test | What it checks | Common methods |
|---|---|---|
| Colour fastness to washing | Fading and staining in the wash | ISO 105-C06, AATCC 61 |
| Colour fastness to rubbing | Colour rubbing off, dry and wet | ISO 105-X12, AATCC 8 |
| Colour fastness to perspiration | Colour change from sweat | ISO 105-E04, AATCC 15 |
| Colour fastness to light | Fading in sunlight | ISO 105-B02, AATCC 16 |
| Dimensional stability | Shrinkage or growth after washing | ISO 6330 with ISO 5077, AATCC 135 and 150 |
| Pilling | Small fibre balls on the surface | ISO 12945-2, ASTM D4970 |
| Seam strength and slippage | Seams that open or slide | ISO 13935, ISO 13936 |
| Fibre content | Fibre percentages on the label | ISO 1833 |
| Chemicals | Restricted substances for your market | Lab methods for each substance |

## Colour fastness test results

A colour fastness test grades two things. Colour change shows how much the garment itself fades. Staining shows how much colour moves onto a white test fabric.

Both use the grey scale rating, from 1 to 5. A 5 means no visible change. Common targets are:

- **Washing:** 4 for colour change, 3 to 4 for staining.
- **Dry rubbing:** 4.
- **Wet rubbing:** 3, or 2 to 3 for deep shades such as black and navy.
- **Light:** often 4 on the blue wool scale for everyday clothing.

Dark colours and bright reactive dyes are the most likely to fail. Our guide to [shade variation](/insights/shade-variation-explained/) covers the related problem of colour differences between pieces.

## Dimensional stability and shrinkage

Dimensional stability measures how much a garment changes size after washing and drying. The lab marks a set distance, washes the piece to a set cycle and measures again.

| Fabric | Typical limit |
|---|---|
| Cotton knits | About 3 to 5% |
| Wovens | About 2 to 3% |
| Twist in knits | Often under 5% twist |

Our free [fabric shrinkage calculator](/tools/shrinkage-calculator/) turns your own wash test into a percentage.

## The pilling test

A pilling test rubs fabric against itself or an abrasive in a machine, then grades the surface from 1 to 5 against photos. Grade 5 means no pilling. Many brands ask for 3 to 4 after a set number of cycles.

Fleece, brushed fabrics and soft blends pill most. Fibre length, yarn twist and anti-pill finishes all help.

## Seams, strength and fibre content

Seam tests check that seams do not burst or slide open under load. Tensile and tear tests check the fabric itself. Fibre content tests confirm the percentages on the care label, which must be accurate by law in most markets.

## Chemical and safety tests

Most markets limit certain substances in clothing, such as azo dyes, formaldehyde, PFAS and some heavy metals. Children's clothing adds safety tests for small parts, drawstrings and flammability. Our guides to [CPSIA compliance for clothing](/insights/cpsia-compliance-guide/) and [EU clothing regulations 2026](/insights/eu-clothing-regulations-2026/) cover the rules.

## When to test

1. **Fabric:** test bulk fabric before cutting, using the mill's report as a first check.
2. **Pre-production sample:** test garments made from bulk fabric and trims.
3. **Bulk:** some brands test pieces pulled from the finished order.

Testing early is cheaper. A failed fabric can be replaced before it becomes a failed shipment.

## Garment testing and inspection

Testing and inspection do different jobs. Testing measures performance in a lab. Inspection checks workmanship, measurements and packing on the finished goods, as our guide to [AQL inspection](/insights/aql-inspection-explained/) explains.
