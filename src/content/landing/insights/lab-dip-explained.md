## What is a lab dip?

What is a lab dip in plain terms? It is a test. Before a factory dyes hundreds of metres of fabric in your brand colour, a lab dyes a small swatch of that same fabric to match your reference. You check it. If it is right, the bulk is dyed to the same recipe.

The swatch you approve becomes the **colour standard**. Every bulk roll is compared with it, and so is every reorder.

Skipping lab dips is the fastest way to end up with a navy that looks purple or a cream that looks yellow.

## Why a Pantone code is not enough

A Pantone code tells the factory which colour you want. It does not tell anyone how that colour will look on your fabric.

- **Fibre changes colour.** Cotton and polyester take different dyes, so one code looks slightly different on each.
- **Fabric structure changes colour.** A smooth jersey and a textured piqué reflect light differently.
- **Paper and fabric differ.** A paper swatch never looks quite the same as dyed cloth.

A lab dip on the real fabric is the only way to see the colour you will actually sell.

## The lab dip process, step by step

1. **Send your reference.** A Pantone textile code, a fabric swatch or a garment to match. Physical swatches give the best first match.
2. **The lab dyes options.** Usually two or three per colour, labelled A, B and C, each slightly different.
3. **The factory checks them.** In a light box, against your reference, before sending.
4. **You judge them.** Under the same light, side by side with your reference.
5. **You approve or comment.** Pick one, or say how the closest one should change.
6. **Re-dip if needed.** The lab adjusts and sends a new round.
7. **The standard is set.** Your approved dip is cut, labelled and kept for bulk and reorders.

The lab dip process repeats for every colour and every fabric in your order. A hoodie in one colour with a matching rib needs dips on both.

## How to judge a lab dip

### Use the right light

Colour changes with light. A dip that matches in daylight can look different under shop lights. This effect is called metamerism.

Judge in a light box if you can, under at least two light sources:

| Light | What it shows |
| --- | --- |
| Daylight (often called D65) | The standard reference for most approvals |
| Shop light (often called TL84 or similar) | How the colour looks in many stores |
| Warm home light | How it looks to the customer at home |

If you have no light box, use north-facing daylight near a window, not direct sun.

### Compare the right way

- Place the dip and your reference side by side, edges touching.
- Look at them flat, then tilt them slightly.
- Swap left and right, because the eye favours one side.
- Look for three things: lighter or darker, more or less bright, and which way the shade leans.

## Delta E tolerance

Instruments measure colour difference as **delta E**, written ΔE. The higher the number, the bigger the difference.

For textiles, the CMC method set out in ISO 105-J03 is widely used. It weights lightness, brightness and hue the way the eye sees them, so one tolerance works across colours.

| CMC 2:1 delta E | What it usually means |
| --- | --- |
| Below 0.5 | Very close, hard to tell apart |
| 0.5 to 1.0 | Close, accepted by many brands for solid colours |
| 1.0 to 2.0 | Visible to a trained eye, often rejected for core colours |
| Above 2.0 | Clearly different |

These bands are common practice, not a rule. Agree your delta E tolerance in writing before bulk, and judge with the eye as well as the instrument.

## How to write lab dip comments

Good lab dip comments save a full round. Poor ones add a week.

| Instead of | Write |
| --- | --- |
| "Wrong colour" | "None close. All too green. Please re-dip towards the reference." |
| "Too dark" | "B closest. Needs to be about 10% lighter, same shade." |
| "Not quite" | "A closest. Slightly redder and slightly duller." |
| "Approved?" | "Approve B under daylight. Use B as the standard." |

Always say which option is closest, which direction it must move, and under which light you judged it.

## Lab dip vs strike-off

These two are often confused.

| | Lab dip | Strike-off |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What it tests | A dyed solid colour | A printed design |
| Made on | The bulk fabric | The bulk fabric, printed |
| You check | Colour only | Colours, sharpness, size and position of the print |
| Used for | Dyed fabric, rib, trims | Screen prints, mill prints, sublimation |

A printed tee in a dyed colour may need both: a lab dip for the fabric and a strike-off for the print.

## Lab dips on reorders

Reorders are where colour drifts. To keep it steady:

- Keep the approved standard, and ask the factory to keep its half.
- Ask for bulk rolls to be checked against the original standard, not the last order.
- Approve a new dip if the fabric mill or base fabric changes.
