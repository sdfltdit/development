## What stitches per inch means

Stitches per inch, shortened to SPI, is the number of stitches in one inch of seam. Count them along a ruler and you have the number.

SPI tells you how dense a seam is. Denser seams are usually stronger and neater. Looser seams are faster to sew but can open, stretch out or look cheap.

Stitches per inch is one of the easiest quality checks a buyer can make. All you need is a ruler.

## SPI chart by garment type

This SPI chart shows common ranges. Your tech pack can set tighter targets.

| Garment or seam | Typical SPI |
| --- | --- |
| Knit tee side seams (overlock) | 10 to 14 |
| Knit hems (coverstitch) | 10 to 12 |
| Woven shirt seams (lockstitch) | 12 to 16 |
| Woven shirt topstitching | 12 to 14 |
| Chinos and twill trousers | 10 to 12 |
| Denim seams and topstitching | 7 to 10 |
| Outerwear and canvas | 8 to 12 |
| Lingerie and fine fabrics | 14 to 18 |

Heavy fabrics use fewer, longer stitches with thicker thread. Fine fabrics need more, shorter stitches with finer thread.

## Stitch types

ISO 4915 classifies stitch types by how threads interlock. Four classes cover most clothing.

| Class | Name | Common example | Where it is used |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 300 | Lockstitch | 301 | Woven seams, topstitching, labels |
| 400 | Chainstitch | 401 | Denim seams, waistbands |
| 500 | Overedge (overlock) | 504, 514, 516 | Knit seams, neatening raw edges |
| 600 | Covering chain | 602, 605, 607 | Knit hems, flatlock seams on sportswear |

### Which stitch for which seam

- **Lockstitch (301)** looks the same on both sides and does not unravel easily. It has little stretch, so it suits wovens.
- **Chainstitch (401)** stretches more and sews fast. It can unravel if a thread breaks, so it is often backed by another seam.
- **Overlock (504, 514)** joins and neatens knit seams in one pass and stretches with the fabric.
- **Safety stitch (516)** combines a chainstitch and an overlock for strong woven seams.
- **Coverstitch (602, 605)** gives the twin-row hem seen on tees and leggings.
- **Flatlock (607)** joins fabric edge to edge for flat, comfortable sportswear seams.

## Stitch density and fabric

Stitch density must suit the fabric:

- **Too low** and seams open, gape or show gaps under strain.
- **Too high** and needles cut fibres, especially on knits, causing holes along the seam later.

The right stitch density also depends on thread and needle size. A heavier thread with fewer stitches can be stronger than a fine thread with many.

## Seam strength

Seam strength is how much force a seam takes before it breaks or slips. It depends on stitch type, SPI, thread, seam allowance and fabric.

| Check | How it is done |
| --- | --- |
| Hand stretch | Pull seams firmly by hand and look for broken or popped stitches |
| Lab pull test | A machine pulls a seam until it fails and records the force |
| Seam slippage | Checks whether woven yarns slide apart beside the seam |
| Wash test | Washes the garment and checks seams for twisting or opening |

Stretch fabrics need stitches that stretch too. A lockstitch on leggings will snap when the wearer bends.

## How to put stitches per inch in your tech pack

Add a construction page with:

1. **Each seam** and its stitch type, such as "side seam: 514 four-thread overlock".
2. **Target SPI** for each, such as "12 plus or minus 1".
3. **Thread** type and size.
4. **Seam allowance** in millimetres.

## Stitch faults to look for

| Fault | What it looks like |
| --- | --- |
| Skipped stitches | Gaps where the needle missed |
| Open seam | Seam pulled apart, stitches broken |
| Puckering | Wavy, gathered seam on flat fabric |
| Uneven stitch length | Stitches long in one place, short in another |
| Raw edges showing | Fabric edge not caught in the overlock |
| Loose thread ends | Untrimmed threads at seam ends |
